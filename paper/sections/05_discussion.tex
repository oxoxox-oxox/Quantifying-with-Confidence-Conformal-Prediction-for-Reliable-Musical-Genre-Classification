\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our experimental results demonstrate that conformal prediction can be
effectively paired with self-supervised audio embeddings to produce
statistically rigorous prediction sets for musical style classification.
In this section, we interpret the theoretical and practical implications of
these findings, discuss limitations of the current approach, and outline
directions for future work.

\subsection{Why Conformal Prediction Works for Musical Style}

The strong performance of CP on the GTZAN dataset is grounded in two
complementary factors. First, the MERT embedding space preserves sufficient
genre-discriminative structure. The measured intra-class cosine similarity of
$0.917$ well above the inter-class value of $0.873$ indicates that same-genre
tracks are systematically closer in the embedding space than across-genre
tracks. This provides a meaningful signal on which the nonconformity score
can operate.

Second, and more fundamentally, the conformal prediction framework makes no
parametric assumptions about the distribution of nonconformity scores. The
empirical disparity between CP and the Gaussian approximation baseline at
$\alpha = 0.01$---where Gaussian achieves only $0.965$ coverage vs.\ CP's
$0.993$---confirms that the cosine-distance-based nonconformity scores exhibit
heavy-tailed behavior. Musical style boundaries are inherently ambiguous:
some tracks sit squarely within a single genre, while others---fusion,
crossover, or experimental works---lie in the stylistic periphery, producing
large nonconformity scores that a thin-tailed Gaussian model cannot account
for. CP gracefully handles this heterogeneity by calibrating on the empirical
distribution directly, without assuming a parametric form.

\subsection{Interpretation of Prediction Set Sizes}

The average prediction set sizes of $8.51$ ($\alpha = 0.01$), $6.69$
($\alpha = 0.05$), and $5.40$ ($\alpha = 0.10$) may appear large relative
to the total of $K = 10$ classes. Several factors contribute to this:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
    \item \textbf{Granularity of the genre taxonomy.} The GTZAN label space
    contains perceptually adjacent genres (e.g., rock, country, pop) whose
    stylistic boundaries are fuzzy by nature. When a track sits near a genre
    boundary, multiple centroids will produce similarly low nonconformity
    scores, leading to larger prediction sets. This is not a deficiency of CP
    but rather an honest reflection of the ambiguity in the label space.

    \item \textbf{Calibration set size.} With $|\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}|
    \approx 200$ tracks (spread across 10 classes), the empirical quantile
    estimate has limited granularity. A well-known property of CP is that
    prediction set size scales roughly as $\mathcal{O}(K / \sqrt{m})$ where
    $m$ is the calibration size. With larger calibration sets, the threshold
    $\hat{q}_{\alpha}$ becomes a more precise estimate, leading to tighter
    prediction sets.

    \item \textbf{Choice of nonconformity score.} The cosine distance
    $A(\mathbf{e}_x, k) = 1 - \cos(\mathbf{e}_x, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)$ is a
    symmetric, class-agnostic score that treats all genres as equally distant.
    An adaptive nonconformity score---for instance, normalizing by the
    intra-class variance of each genre centroid---could produce class-conditional
    thresholds and potentially reduce set sizes for ``tight'' genres while
    increasing them for diffuse ones.
\end{itemize}

The heterogeneity of individual set sizes---ranging from singletons (high
confidence) to the full set of 10 (high ambiguity)---suggests that CP-based
prediction sets carry interpretable information beyond aggregate coverage.
A track assigned a singleton set $\{\text{metal}\}$ is a robust classification;
a track receiving all 10 genres signals that it is stylistically ambiguous
or out-of-distribution relative to the calibration data.

\subsection{CP vs. Bootstrap and Gaussian Baselines}

The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:coverage_results} reveals a clear ordering
in terms of statistical validity:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
    \item \textbf{CP} provides the only distribution-free, finite-sample
    guarantee and achieves empirical coverage that tightly matches nominal
    levels at all $\alpha$.

    \item \textbf{Bootstrap} is empirically competitive on the GTZAN dataset
    but offers no theoretical guarantee for finite samples. Its computational
    cost ($1{,}000$ resamples of the calibration set) is prohibitive for
    real-time applications or large-scale deployment.

    \item \textbf{Gaussian approximation} fails at the high-confidence
    tail ($\alpha = 0.01$), where its normality assumption does not hold.
    While computationally trivial (a single fitted normal distribution), its
    undercoverage in the high-stakes regime renders it unsafe for applications
    requiring reliable confidence bounds.
\end{enumerate}

The bootstrap's proximity to CP at $\alpha = 0.05$ and $\alpha = 0.10$
indicates that with sufficiently many samples, both the empirical quantile
and the bootstrap distribution converge to the true quantile. The bootstrap's
slight undercoverage at $\alpha = 0.01$ ($0.987$ vs.\ target $0.990$) may
reflect finite-sample bias in the bootstrap quantile estimate for extreme
tail probabilities.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations of the current work warrant discussion.

\textbf{Single-dataset evaluation.} Our experiments are confined to the GTZAN
dataset, which, despite its canonical status, has known limitations: short
30-second excerpts, a coarse 10-genre taxonomy, and potential artifacts
(introduced by the encoding pipeline) that models may exploit rather than
learning genuine musical features. Evaluation on larger and more diverse
datasets---such as the Free Music Archive (FMA)~\cite{defferrard2017fma}---is
an important next step.

\textbf{Calibration-test exchangeability.} The coverage guarantee of CP
requires that calibration and test data be exchangeable. In practice, this
assumption may be violated if the test distribution shifts---for instance,
when evaluating AI-generated music that may occupy regions of the embedding
space not represented in the GTZAN calibration data. Distribution shift is a
known challenge for all CP methods, and detecting when the exchangeability
assumption fails is an active research area.

\textbf{MERT embedding layer selection.} We use only the last hidden layer
of the MERT-v1-330M encoder. Prior work has shown that different layers encode
qualitatively different musical information: lower layers capture fine-grained
acoustic and timbral detail, while higher layers encode more abstract
structural and semantic features. A weighted combination or learned selection
of layers could improve discriminability and reduce prediction set sizes.

\textbf{Computational overhead.} While the MERT embedding extraction is a
one-time cost ($\sim$9~minutes for GTZAN), the mean-pooling over 10-second
segments with 5-second overlap is a heuristic choice. End-to-end models that
ingest variable-length audio directly---or more efficient segment aggregation
strategies---could reduce the extraction overhead for large-scale datasets.

\subsection{Future Work}

The present work establishes a baseline for conformal prediction in musical
style assessment. Several extensions are natural:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
    \item \textbf{Adaptive nonconformity scores.} Designing class-conditional
    scores that account for intra-genre variance could yield tighter prediction
    sets. For example, normalizing the cosine distance by the empirical
    standard deviation of each centroid: $A_{\text{adapt}}(\mathbf{e}_x, k) =
    (1 - \cos(\mathbf{e}_x, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)) / \hat{\sigma}_k$.

    \item \textbf{Confidence-guided filtering of generated music.} A direct
    application of our framework is post-filtering AI-generated music.
    Given a target style, tracks whose prediction set does not include
    the target genre---or whose $p$-value for the target genre falls below
    a threshold---can be rejected with statistical justification.

    \item \textbf{Multimodal style assessment.} Integrating textual metadata
    (lyrics, artist descriptions) with audio embeddings---for instance, via
    CLAP-style joint embeddings~\cite{elizalde2022clap}---could enrich the
    stylistic representation and narrow prediction sets for ambiguous cases.

    \item \textbf{Distribution-shift detection.} Pairing CP prediction sets
    with a test for exchangeability violations would enable the framework to
    signal when its coverage guarantee may not hold, enhancing trustworthiness
    in deployment scenarios with evolving musical content.
\end{itemize}
