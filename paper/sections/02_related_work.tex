\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Our work sits at the intersection of three research areas: (i)~self-supervised
audio representation learning for music, (ii)~conformal prediction for
distribution-free uncertainty quantification, and (iii)~evaluation of musical
style and generative models. We review each in turn and identify the gap that
our framework fills.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Audio Representation Learning for Music}

The past five years have witnessed a paradigm shift from hand-crafted audio
features---such as MFCCs, chroma vectors, and spectral contrast---to
deep learned representations obtained via self-supervised pre-training.
This transition began in the speech domain with Wav2Vec~2.0~\cite{baevski2020wav2vec2},
which jointly learns discrete speech units and contextualized representations
through contrastive learning over masked frame prediction. HuBERT~\cite{hsu2021hubert}
extended this approach by replacing the quantized latent codes with discrete
targets generated by offline $k$-means clustering of MFCC features, yielding
stronger representations for downstream tasks.

For music, however, the challenge is more complex: music signals carry richer
acoustic and structural information---timbre, harmony, rhythm, and genre
conventions---that speech-oriented models do not fully capture.
Early efforts adapted vision-style transfer learning to audio, using
convolutional neural networks trained on music tagging tasks~\cite{choi2017transfer}.
Castellon et al.~\cite{castellon2021codified} proposed codified audio language
modeling, which tokenizes audio into discrete units and applies a BERT-style
masked language modeling objective, showing promise for general-purpose music
representation learning.

\textbf{MERT} (Music undERstanding model with large-scale self-supervised
Training)~\cite{li2023mert} represents the state of the art in self-supervised
music representation. Its architecture builds on the Wav2Vec~2.0 backbone---a
multi-layer Transformer encoder with convolutional feature extraction---but
introduces a novel combination of two complementary teachers for its masked
language modeling pre-training objective:
(1)~an \textbf{acoustic teacher} based on Residual Vector Quantization VAE
(RVQ-VAE), following EnCodec~\cite{defossez2022encodec}, which provides
fine-grained acoustic pseudo-labels capturing timbral and spectral detail; and
(2)~a \textbf{musical teacher} based on the Constant-Q Transform (CQT), which
supplies harmonic and pitch-structure supervision. By jointly optimizing under
both teachers on 160,000 hours of music, MERT learns representations that
simultaneously encode low-level acoustic texture and high-level musical
structure. Two model sizes are available: MERT-v1-95M (12-layer Transformer,
768-dimensional embeddings, 20K hours) and MERT-v1-330M (24-layer Transformer,
1024-dimensional embeddings, 160K hours), the latter being the variant used in
this work.

In parallel, contrastive language-audio models such as
CLAP~\cite{elizalde2022clap} and its large-scale extension
LAION-CLAP~\cite{wu2023laionclap} learn joint audio-text embeddings that
enable zero-shot music classification and retrieval via natural language
queries. While effective for semantic tasks, these multimodal embeddings are
optimized for language alignment and may not capture purely acoustic stylistic
nuance as faithfully as unimodal music SSL models. For this reason, we adopt
MERT as the embedding backbone for our style similarity framework.

\subsection{Musical Style Similarity and Evaluation}

Quantifying musical style similarity has been a long-standing challenge in
MIR. Traditional approaches operate on hand-crafted features: Tzanetakis and
Cook~\cite{tzanetakis2002gtzan} established the GTZAN benchmark using
timbre, rhythm, and pitch features for genre classification, and
subsequent work explored compression-based distance measures and
content-based feature statistics. While interpretable, these
shallow representations struggle to capture the high-level perceptual
attributes that define musical style.

Deep learning brought substantial progress: features extracted from
pre-trained deep networks, such as music auto-tagging CNNs~\cite{choi2017transfer},
consistently outperformed hand-crafted features on style-related tasks.
More recently, the MERT model has set new state-of-the-art results across
numerous MIR benchmarks including genre classification, emotion recognition,
instrument tagging, and key detection, demonstrating that large-scale SSL
produces embeddings that are broadly useful for style-related downstream
tasks.

For generative model evaluation, several metrics have been widely adopted.
The Fr\'{e}chet Audio Distance (FAD)~\cite{kilgour2019fad} computes the
Fr\'{e}chet distance between Gaussian approximations of real and generated
audio embeddings, providing a distribution-level quality measure. Yang and
Lerch~\cite{yang2018evaluation} surveyed multiple evaluation strategies for
generative music models, including likelihood-based scoring and statistical
feature comparisons, and highlighted the lack of per-sample confidence
measures as a critical shortcoming. MusicGen~\cite{copet2023musicgen} and
AudioLDM~2~\cite{liu2023audioldm2} further illustrate the need for
fine-grained, instance-level quality assessment during generation and
post-filtering.

A critical observation across all these evaluation paradigms is that they
produce \emph{point estimates}: a single similarity score, a single FAD value,
or a single classification label, with no accompanying measure of uncertainty.
When a generated track receives a similarity score of 0.78, there is no way
to know whether this estimate is precise (narrow confidence) or unreliable
(wide confidence). This gap is precisely what our conformal prediction
framework addresses.

\subsection{Conformal Prediction}

Conformal Prediction (CP)~is a distribution-free, finite-sample framework for
constructing prediction sets with rigorous statistical coverage guarantees.
The theoretical foundation was laid by Vovk et al.~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic},
and a comprehensive tutorial was provided by
Shafer and Vovk~\cite{shafer2008tutorial}. A modern, accessible introduction
is given by Angelopoulos and Bates~\cite{angelopoulos2023gentle}, who survey
the algorithmic foundations and diverse applications of CP.

In the most widely used variant, \emph{inductive} (or split) conformal
prediction, the available labeled data is partitioned into a \emph{training
set} (used to fit the underlying model) and a \emph{calibration set} (used
to determine the prediction set's size). Given a test input $x$, the framework
produces a prediction set $\Gamma(x) \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$ such that
$\mathbb{P}(Y_{\text{test}} \in \Gamma(X_{\text{test}})) \geq 1 - \alpha$,
where the probability is taken over the random draw of both calibration and
test data. This guarantee requires only the exchangeability of the data---a
condition satisfied, in particular, by i.i.d.\ draws.

The construction of $\Gamma(x)$ proceeds via a \emph{nonconformity score}
$A(x, y)$, a real-valued function that measures how unusual a candidate label
$y$ is for a given input $x$. After computing $A_i$ for each calibration
point $(X_i, Y_i)$, the $\lceil (|\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}| + 1)(1 - \alpha)
\rceil$-th largest score serves as a threshold $\hat{q}_{\alpha}$. The
prediction set is then $\Gamma(x) = \{ y \in \mathcal{Y} : A(x, y) \leq
\hat{q}_{\alpha} \}$.

CP has been applied successfully across a wide range of domains.
Romano et al.~\cite{romano2019conformalized} extended the framework to
regression via Conformalized Quantile Regression, providing adaptive
prediction intervals whose width varies with input difficulty.
Angelopoulos et al.~\cite{angelopoulos2021uncertainty} applied CP to image
classification, constructing class-conditional prediction sets that achieve
theoretical coverage while remaining practically compact. The MAPIE
library~\cite{cordier2023mapie} provides a standardized software
implementation of CP methods for the Python ecosystem.

Despite this breadth of application, CP has seen relatively little use in the
audio and music domains. In particular, no prior work has applied conformal
prediction to the problem of musical style similarity assessment using
self-supervised embeddings. Our work fills this gap by designing a CP
framework tailored to the structure of MERT embeddings and genre-based
similarity scoring.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification in Music Information Retrieval}

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) in MIR remains under-explored relative to
other machine learning domains such as computer vision and medical diagnosis.
Existing approaches fall broadly into two categories: Bayesian methods
(including Monte Carlo dropout and deep ensembles) and bootstrap-based
resampling. Bayesian approaches estimate posterior predictive distributions
over model parameters, providing uncertainty estimates at the cost of
computational overhead and sensitivity to prior specification. Bootstrap
methods approximate sampling distributions through repeated re-sampling of
the training data, but offer only asymptotic guarantees and can be
prohibitively expensive for large models such as MERT.

Critically, neither Bayesian nor bootstrap approaches provide the
\emph{distribution-free, finite-sample} coverage guarantees that conformal
prediction offers. In musical style assessment, where the underlying
similarity scores often exhibit heavy-tailed and multi-modal distributions
that violate standard parametric assumptions, this advantage is particularly
salient. Our experiments (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) empirically
demonstrate that a Gaussian approximation baseline yields coverage of only
$0.965$ at $\alpha = 0.01$ (vs.\ the nominal $0.99$), while CP achieves
$0.993$, underscoring the practical value of distribution-free guarantees
in this domain.

To the best of our knowledge, the present work represents the first application
of conformal prediction to musical style similarity quantification. We aim to
demonstrate that CP not only provides theoretical rigor but also yields
practically useful prediction sets that can improve downstream tasks such as
AI-generated music filtering and quality assessment.
