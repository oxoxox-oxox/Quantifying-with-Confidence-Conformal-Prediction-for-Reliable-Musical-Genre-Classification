\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a framework that integrates self-supervised audio embeddings
with conformal prediction to produce statistically rigorous prediction sets
for musical style similarity assessment. By extracting genre-discriminative
1024-dimensional embeddings from MERT-v1-330M, constructing prototype
centroids from a reference set, and calibrating nonconformity scores on a
held-out calibration set, our method produces prediction sets
$\Gamma_{\alpha}(x)$ that satisfy the marginal coverage guarantee
$\mathbb{P}(y_{\text{test}} \in \Gamma_{\alpha}(x)) \geq 1 - \alpha$ under
the sole assumption of exchangeability.

Our experiments on the GTZAN dataset demonstrate that this guarantee holds in
practice. At significance levels $\alpha = 0.01$, $0.05$, and $0.10$,
conformal prediction achieves empirical coverages of $0.993$, $0.955$, and
$0.910$, respectively, all tightly matching or exceeding the nominal targets.
In contrast, a Gaussian approximation baseline---the default uncertainty
quantification method in many machine learning pipelines---undercovers
substantially at high confidence ($0.965$ vs.\ target $0.99$ at
$\alpha = 0.01$), confirming that the heavy-tailed nature of perceptual
similarity distributions renders parametric assumptions unsafe. Bootstrap
resampling, while empirically competitive, lacks the finite-sample validity
guarantee that distinguishes CP and incurs approximately three orders of
magnitude higher computational cost.

The average prediction set sizes ($8.51$, $6.69$, and $5.40$ at
$\alpha = 0.01$, $0.05$, and $0.10$, respectively) reflect a principled
trade-off: CP pays an ``uncertainty premium'' in set size to deliver its
distribution-free coverage guarantee, and our analysis of the GTZAN label
space suggests that a substantial portion of this premium is attributable to
genuine stylistic ambiguity among perceptually adjacent genres rather than
to any deficiency of the method.

We believe this work opens several avenues for future research. The
application of CP to filtering and quality assessment of AI-generated music
is a natural next step, with the coverage guarantee providing a formal
justification for rejection decisions. Extending the framework to multimodal
style representations (audio + text) and designing adaptive nonconformity
scores that account for intra-genre variability could yield tighter prediction
sets. Finally, as self-supervised music models continue to scale in
capacity and training data, the embedding spaces they produce will grow
increasingly discriminative, further narrowing the gap between coverage
guarantee and practical informativeness.

In summary, quantifying musical style similarity with confidence is both
feasible and principled. Conformal prediction bridges the gap between the
rich representational power of self-supervised audio models and the
statistical rigor required for trustworthy downstream decision-making in
music information retrieval.
